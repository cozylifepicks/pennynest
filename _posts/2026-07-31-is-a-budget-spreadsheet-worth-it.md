## 11 things to know before you buy one

### 1. It only helps if your problem is visibility

Most people underestimate their spending by a wide margin — not by lying to
themselves, but because small payments never register as events. A spreadsheet's
real job is to make a month visible on one screen. If you already know your
numbers to the euro and still overspend, buy the envelopes instead.

### 2. The one-time price matters more than it sounds

Budgeting apps mostly charge monthly. A spreadsheet is a one-time payment (this
one sits under $40), and it keeps working whether or not you renew anything. Over
a couple of years that difference is real money — which is a slightly funny thing
to ignore when the whole point is watching where money goes.

### 3. Google Sheets means no software to install

It opens in a browser and syncs to your phone automatically. You do need a free
Google account, and you make your own copy of the sheet — after that it is
yours, offline exportable, and nobody can switch off the feature you liked.

### 4. Check how it arrives before you buy

This one is delivered as a small PDF containing your download link, which trips
people up if they expect the sheet itself in their inbox. It is a normal digital
delivery, but knowing it in advance saves a confused evening.

### 5. Automatic bank syncing is the thing you give up

This is the honest trade. An app pulls transactions in; a spreadsheet asks you to
enter them. Ten minutes a week, roughly. Some people hate that. Others find that
the typing *is* the budgeting — you notice the third takeaway when you have to
write it down yourself.

### 6. A dashboard changes the conversation

Rows of numbers start arguments; a chart ends them. The interactive summary is
the part most people actually use after week one, because it answers "can we
afford this?" in about four seconds.

### 7. Look for yearly history, not just monthly

A budget that resets every month hides your progress. Being able to see January
next to July is what turns budgeting from a chore into something mildly
addictive — you can finally see the line moving.

### 8. Customisable beats "complete"

No template matches your life out of the box. The useful question is whether you
can rename categories and add rows without breaking the formulas. If a planner
locks its structure, skip it.

### 9. Set it up on a payday, not a Sunday night

Whatever you buy, set it up the day money lands, while the numbers are fresh and
you are not tired. This is the single biggest predictor of whether a budget
survives its first month — more than the tool.

## Who should honestly skip it

Skip it if you are already using a system that works — the best budget is the
one you keep opening. Skip it if your income is so irregular that monthly
categories are meaningless; a simple running balance serves you better. And skip

## How to make it stick in the first week

Set aside one evening. Copy the last 30 days of transactions from your bank app
into the sheet — all of them, including the embarrassing ones. Name your
categories after your real life, not accounting terms ("dog", "coffee",
"mum's birthday"). Then set a 10-minute weekly slot in your calendar and treat
it like a bill. Four weeks of that is where the payoff shows up.

## Common questions

### Is a paid budget spreadsheet better than a free template?

Usually yes, but not magically. Free templates are static grids; a paid dynamic
planner does the allocation, the summaries and the charts for you. You are
paying for the formulas and the layout, not the idea.

### Do I need to know spreadsheet formulas?

No. A well-built planner asks you to type numbers into labelled cells and does
everything else. If a product expects you to write formulas yourself, it is a
template, not a planner.

### Will it work on my phone?

Yes — Google Sheets has free mobile apps for both iPhone and Android, and your
sheet syncs automatically. Entering expenses on the phone as they happen is the
habit that makes the weekly review take five minutes instead of forty.

### What if I already tried budgeting and failed?

Then you are the intended reader. Budgets rarely fail from bad maths; they fail
from friction. Cut the friction — fewer categories, one weekly slot, a tool that
does the arithmetic — and try again. Our

starts from exactly there.
