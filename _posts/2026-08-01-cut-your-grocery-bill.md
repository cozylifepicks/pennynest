## 1. Never shop hungry — it's the most expensive mistake

This is not a cliché, it's a measurable one: hungry shoppers buy more, and buy
more of the expensive impulse things at the front of the store. Eat something
first, even a slice of toast in the car. It is the single cheapest change on
this list and it works the first time you do it.

## 2. Write the list from what you already have

Start at the fridge and cupboards, not at a recipe. Most households buy a third
pack of pasta while three sit at the back of a shelf. Doing a two-minute stock
check first is what stops the slow accumulation of duplicates that quietly eats
$40 a month.

## 4. Cook five meals you actually like, on rotation

Ambitious meal plans die in week two, and the food you bought for them dies in
the bin. Pick five meals your household genuinely enjoys, learn to buy for them
without thinking, and rotate. Boring is cheap, and boring is what you'll
actually cook on a Tuesday.

## 6. Use the freezer as a savings account

The freezer is what lets you buy meat and bread when they're marked down
instead of when you need them. Portion things the day you buy them with
[freezer storage containers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=freezer+storage+containers+set&tag=tamirugc20-20)

## 7. Switch the five things you buy every week

You don't need to change your whole shop — just the items that appear on every
receipt. Moving the store-brand version of milk, bread, pasta, rice and tinned
tomatoes typically saves $15–$25 a week with no noticeable difference. Keep the
brands you genuinely taste; swap the ones you don't.

## 8. Shop once a week, not "on the way home"

Every extra trip is an extra chance to impulse-buy, and three small shops
almost always cost more than one planned one. If you run out of something
mid-week, write it down and wait. The gap is where most of the saving happens.

## 9. Check the unit price, not the shelf price

The bigger pack is not automatically cheaper, and supermarkets know you assume
it is. The small print on the shelf label gives price per kilo or per litre —
comparing that one number takes three seconds and regularly reveals that the
"family size" is the more expensive option.

## 10. Give groceries a cash limit

If groceries are the category that always overruns, take the week's budget out
in cash. A
[cash envelope binder](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cash+envelope+system+budget+binder&tag=tamirugc20-20)
makes the limit physical: when the envelope is empty, the week's shopping is
done. Card spending is invisible until the statement, and by then it's spent.

## 11. Waste less, because waste is money you already spent

Households routinely throw away close to a fifth of the food they buy. Keep a
"eat me first" shelf at eye level in the fridge for anything close to turning,
and plan one meal a week designed to use leftovers. This costs nothing and
recovers a real percentage of the bill.

## 12. Buy the boring staples in bulk, nothing else

Rice, oats, tinned goods, pasta and cleaning products are worth buying big —
they don't spoil and the unit price is genuinely lower. Fresh food in bulk is a
false economy, because the savings end up in the bin. Bulk only what keeps.

## 13. Bank the difference or it disappears

This is the step almost everyone skips. If you cut $40 from this week's shop,
that $40 stays in your current account and gets absorbed by something else
within days. Move it to savings the same day. A saving you don't transfer is
just a slower way of spending.

You don't need to do all thirteen. Shop with a list and not hungry, cook once
and eat three times, and move what you save the same day — those three alone
take a typical grocery bill down by a noticeable amount within one month.

## Common questions

### How much can you realistically save on groceries?

Most households cut 15–25% without eating worse, which is roughly $100–$300 a
month depending on family size. The savings come mostly from fewer trips, less
waste and store-brand staples — not from hunting bargains, which costs more
time than it returns.

### Is meal planning necessary to save money on food?

No. A rotation of five meals you already like does the same job with far less
effort, and it survives a bad week. Detailed weekly meal plans have a high
failure rate, and the food bought for an abandoned plan is pure waste.

### Do grocery savings apps actually work?

They help a little, but they're the smallest lever here. Shopping once a week
instead of three times, and not shopping hungry, save more than any app —
and they're free. Fix the habits first, then add tools if you still want to.

### What's the fastest way to see where my food money goes?

Add up the last 30 days of grocery transactions in your bank app, or drop them
into a [budget spreadsheet](https://www.digital-planning-studio.com/#aff=tamirjr)
that separates food from everything else. Almost everyone finds the number is
higher than they'd have guessed, which is exactly why it's the easiest bill to cut.
