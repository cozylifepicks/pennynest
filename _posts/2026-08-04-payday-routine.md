Payday is the best day of the month and the most dangerous one. The balance
looks healthy, the pressure lifts, and for about seventy-two hours everything
feels fine — then the direct debits land, the weekend happens, and by the
second week you're back to checking the app before buying coffee.

The fix isn't more willpower later in the month. It's a routine on the day
itself. Money that has been given a job on payday doesn't get spent twice, and
the whole thing takes about twenty minutes once a month. Here are the twelve
steps, in the order we actually do them.

## 1. Do it the day the money lands, not the weekend after

The routine loses most of its power if you wait. Between payday and Saturday,
a surprising amount leaves on its own — a delivery, a round of drinks, a
"we deserve this" dinner. Doing the whole thing within a few hours of the money
arriving means you're dividing up the full amount rather than the leftovers.

## 2. Pay the bills money out first

Add up every fixed cost that will hit before the next payday — rent, utilities,
insurance, subscriptions, loan payments — and move that exact total into a
separate account the same day. Not mentally set aside. Actually moved. Money
sitting in your everyday account is money you will spend, however firmly you
have promised yourself otherwise.

## 3. Send savings before you send anything else

The classic advice is "pay yourself first" and it's classic because it survives
contact with real life. Whatever you plan to save, move it on payday, not at the
end of the month out of what's left — because there is never anything left.
Start smaller than feels impressive: a sum you won't reverse next week beats an
ambitious one you cancel.

## 4. Know the number you're actually dividing up

Most payday routines fail on arithmetic, not discipline. If you don't know what
your fixed costs really total, every month is a guess and every guess runs
short. Pull the last two months of transactions and total them by category
once. A

does this in one screen and turns payday into filling in known amounts.

## 5. Give the leftover a name

The money that remains after bills and savings is your actual spending money,
and it behaves completely differently once it has a name. "$640 for the month"
is abstract. "$160 a week for food, gas and fun" is a number you can check
against on a Tuesday. Divide the leftover by the number of weeks until the next
payday and treat that as the real limit.

## 6. Take out cash for the categories that always overrun

Everyone has two or three categories where the leak happens — usually
groceries, eating out and small impulse buys. Those are the ones worth pulling
out in cash on payday, because a card gives you no signal until the statement.

## 7. Handle the annual bills in twelfths

Car insurance, road tax, Christmas, the boiler service — these aren't
emergencies, they're appointments you already know about, and they wreck more
budgets than genuine surprises do. Add them up, divide by twelve, and move that
amount on payday into a separate pot. The month the bill lands stops being a
crisis and becomes a transfer.

## 8. Automate the transfers you repeat every month

Anything you do identically every payday should happen without you. Set the
standing orders for bills, savings and the annual-bills pot to run the day after
payday. Automation isn't about laziness — it removes the twelve chances a year
you'd otherwise have to skip it "just this once".

## 9. Check last month before planning this one

Spend five minutes looking at where the money actually went. Not to feel bad
about it — to correct the amounts. Overspending the same category three months
running is information: the number was wrong, not you. Adjust it up and take
the difference from somewhere you genuinely don't miss.

## 10. Keep a small buffer in the current account

Leave a modest float — enough that a mistimed direct debit doesn't cause a
charge. Overdraft and late fees are the most expensive money in most people's
lives, and they usually happen for want of a small cushion rather than any real
shortage.

## 11. Deal with the "payday feeling" on purpose

The urge to celebrate on payday is real, and pretending it isn't is why strict
budgets collapse. Give it a line and a limit: a set treat amount, taken out on
the day with everything else. Planned spending doesn't damage a budget. It's
the unplanned version of exactly the same purchase that does.

## 12. Do the whole thing again next month, badly if necessary

A payday routine done imperfectly for twelve months beats a perfect one done
twice. Some months you'll be travelling, or ill, or the pay date will move and
everything will slide. Do the two steps that matter — bills out, savings out —
and let the rest go. Consistency is doing most of the routine most of the time.

## Common questions

### What should I do first on payday?

Move the money for fixed bills into a separate account, then move your savings.
Those two transfers do most of the work, and they take about ten minutes. Every
other step in this routine is a refinement of what's left over once those two
have happened.

### Why does my paycheck disappear in three days?

Almost always because it's all sitting in one account with no job assigned to
it. Spending naturally expands to fill the balance you can see, so a healthy-
looking current account on payday gets treated as spending money — right up
until the direct debits land and reveal that it wasn't.

### How much should I save on payday if money is tight?

Less than you think, and reliably. An amount you can move every single month
without reversing it builds the habit, and the habit is what compounds. Raising
it later is easy; restarting after you've cancelled three months in a row is
much harder.

### Does a payday routine work if I'm paid weekly or irregularly?

Yes, with one change: budget on your lowest typical month rather than your
average. Run the same routine on each payment, scaled down, and treat anything
above your baseline as a windfall to be assigned deliberately rather than
absorbed into normal spending.
