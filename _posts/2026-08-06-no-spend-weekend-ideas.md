Most budgets don't break on the big things. They break between Friday evening
and Sunday night, in twenty-pound pieces — a takeaway because nobody wants to
cook, a coffee out because you're already in town, a "quick shop" that comes to
sixty. Nothing in that list feels reckless. Together they're often the single
largest discretionary line in the month.

A no-spend weekend is the cheapest experiment in personal finance: two days,
no cost, and you find out exactly where your money actually goes. The version
that works isn't a weekend of sitting in the dark feeling deprived. It's a
weekend that's been *planned* instead of improvised, because almost every
weekend pound is spent solving a problem you could have solved on Thursday.

## 1. Decide on Thursday, not Saturday morning

A no-spend weekend declared on Saturday at 10am has already lost. By then
you're hungry, the fridge is a question mark and the easiest answer costs
money. Pick the weekend two or three days ahead and spend ten minutes on
Thursday working out what you'll eat and what you'll do. That ten minutes is
the whole trick — everything below is just detail.

## 2. Shop the cupboards before you shop anything else

Almost every household is carrying a week of food it has forgotten about: pasta,
tins, a bag of rice, half a freezer. Before the weekend, pull it all out where
you can see it and build two dinners from what's there. It's the same exercise
that cuts a grocery bill permanently — see
[13 ways to cut your grocery bill](/cut-your-grocery-bill/) — just compressed
into a weekend.

## 4. Plan the entertainment, or the entertainment plans you

Boredom is expensive. An empty Saturday afternoon reliably turns into a trip
somewhere that sells things. Decide in advance what you're actually doing:
a walk with a destination, a film at home, a long lunch with friends where
everyone brings something. A
[board game](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=family+board+games+for+adults&tag=tamirugc20-20)
that gets played fifty times is one of the few genuinely cheap-per-hour things
you can own.

## 5. Leave the cards at home when you go out

If you're going for a walk, a run or a swim, take a key and a phone and nothing
else. Most weekend impulse spending is genuinely impulsive — it happens because
paying takes four seconds and requires no decision. Reintroducing a small amount
of friction removes most of it without any willpower at all.

## 6. Use the free version of the thing you were going to pay for

Libraries lend books, films, and often museum passes. Councils run free events
nobody hears about. Parks, beaches, canal paths and hills are free, permanently.
The gap between a paid Saturday and a free one is usually about fifteen minutes
of looking things up, and that's on Thursday too.

## 7. Do the job you keep paying someone else to avoid

There's usually one — the haircut you could stretch another two weeks, the car
you keep taking through the wash, the shelf you've been meaning to put up. A
no-spend weekend has the one resource a normal weekend doesn't: unbooked time.
Using it on a thing that would otherwise cost money counts twice.

## 8. Cook once, eat three times

Sunday's expensive because everyone's tired by then. Cook a big batch of
something on Saturday — a chilli, a curry, a tray of roast vegetables — and
Sunday's meals become reheating rather than a decision. This is also the single
habit that stops the Monday-to-Wednesday takeaway, so the saving outlives the
weekend.

## 9. Tell the people you'd normally spend with

Half the pressure to spend at the weekend is social, and almost all of it
evaporates when you say it out loud. "We're doing a no-spend weekend, come over
instead" works far more often than it fails. The friends who mind are the
expensive ones anyway.

## 10. Put the money you didn't spend somewhere you can see it

This is the step almost everyone skips and it's the one that makes the habit
stick. Work out roughly what a normal weekend costs you, and on Sunday night
move that amount into savings. Otherwise the money quietly gets absorbed into
the week and it feels like you did it for nothing. If you'd rather do it in
cash, the [cash envelope method](/cash-envelope-method/) is the physical version.

## 11. Expect the first one to be uncomfortable

The first no-spend weekend usually reveals how much of your leisure time is
built around buying something, and that's an odd thing to notice about yourself.
It gets easier immediately. By the third one it stops feeling like an exercise
and starts feeling like an ordinary weekend that happens to be free.

## 12. Do one a month rather than four in a row

Four no-spend weekends back to back is a diet, and it ends the way diets end.
One a month is sustainable, saves a meaningful amount over a year, and — more
usefully — resets your sense of what a weekend has to cost. The months you
need it most, do two.

## Common questions

### What counts as spending on a no-spend weekend?

Set the rules before you start, and keep them simple: no discretionary spending,
but existing bills, medicine, fuel to get to work and genuine emergencies don't
count. Arguing on Saturday about whether something qualifies is how the whole
thing collapses. Write the three exceptions down on Thursday.

### How much does a no-spend weekend actually save?

For most households, somewhere between $60 and $150 a weekend, which is $700 to
$1,800 a year at one a month. The precise figure matters less than seeing it:
add up your last four weekends from your bank app and the number is usually
higher than the guess.

### Isn't it easier to just budget better?

They solve different problems. A budget tells you what you can afford; a
no-spend weekend shows you what you were spending without deciding to. Most
people find the second one changes behaviour faster — see
[how to budget when you're bad with money](/how-to-budget-when-bad-with-money/)
for the version that runs all month.

### What if I have kids?

It's usually easier, not harder. Children rate a den, a park and a film night
extremely highly and have no view on what it cost. The expensive weekends with
kids are the unplanned ones, where the day out gets invented at 11am and
includes lunch, parking and a gift shop.
