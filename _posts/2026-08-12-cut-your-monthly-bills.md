Most advice about spending less is about the fun stuff — the coffee, the
takeaway, the impulse buys. But the money that leaves quietly every month, on
the same date, from the same account, is usually the bigger number. Nobody
enjoys a broadband bill. Nobody feels deprived when it goes down.

That's what makes fixed bills the best place to start. Cutting one takes an
afternoon at most, and unlike a spending diet it keeps paying every single
month without any willpower. Here are the twelve we'd work through, roughly in
order of how much they tend to give back for the effort.

## 1. Write down every recurring payment, in one list

Before cutting anything, get the full picture. Go through the last three months
of statements and write down every payment that repeats — not just the obvious
ones. Three months matters because quarterly and annual charges hide from a
single month's view.

Almost everyone finds something: a trial that converted, a service billed to a
card they've replaced, a subscription for an app they stopped opening in
spring. That list is the whole job. Everything below is just working down it.

## 2. Cancel the one you already know about

There is usually one. The gym you drive past, the streaming service nobody has
opened since the series ended, the cloud storage you pay for twice. You've
thought about cancelling it several times and never quite done it.

Do that one first, today, before the rest of the list. It's the cheapest
possible win and it makes the other eleven steps feel worth starting.

## 3. Call and ask for the retention price

This is the highest-value phone call most households can make. Broadband,
mobile, insurance and TV providers nearly all price new customers better than
existing ones, and the gap doesn't close on its own — it widens every year you
stay quiet.

Look up what a new customer pays for your exact package, call, and say you're
thinking of leaving over the price. Ask to be put through to the team that
handles cancellations, because that's usually where the real discounts live.
Fifteen minutes, no change to your service.

## 4. Never let an introductory rate roll over

Introductory pricing is designed around the fact that most people don't act
when it ends. The rate quietly doubles and the bill blends back into normal
life.

The fix costs nothing: whenever you take a deal, put its end date in your
calendar with a reminder a month before. Future you gets a nudge to renegotiate
while you still have leverage rather than three months after the increase.

## 5. Re-shop insurance instead of renewing it

Car, home and contents insurance are the classic loyalty penalty. Auto-renewal
is convenient and it is very rarely the best price available to you.

Get two or three fresh quotes before each renewal, then take the best one back
to your current insurer. Keep the cover level identical while you compare, or
you're not comparing anything — a cheaper policy with a much higher excess is a
different product, not a saving.

## 6. Match your phone plan to what you actually use

Most people pay for a data allowance built around the heaviest month they ever
had. Check the last six months of actual usage in your account, then pick the
plan that covers your normal month with a little headroom.

If your handset is paid off, check whether you're still on a plan that includes
the cost of it. Paying a phone off twice is one of the most common invisible
bills there is.

## 7. Find the appliances that are quietly expensive

Energy bills feel fixed because they arrive as one number, but they're the sum
of a lot of small decisions. An old second fridge in the garage, a tumble dryer
used out of habit, or a hot water setting nobody has touched in years can be a
meaningful share of the total.

Some bills are high for boringly physical reasons. A dripping hot tap, a draught

## 9. Pay annually only when it genuinely costs less

Annual billing is often 10–20% cheaper than monthly, which makes it a real
saving on things you're certain to keep for a year. It is a trap on everything
else, because it removes the monthly moment where you notice you don't use it.

Rule of thumb: pay annually for the two or three services you'd re-buy without
hesitating, and keep everything else monthly so it stays easy to cancel.

## 10. Strip the add-ons nobody chose

Bills accumulate extras: line rental for a landline with no handset attached,
insurance on a device you no longer own, "priority support" you've never used,
a warranty that duplicates cover you already have.

These are rarely cancelled because they're small individually and invisible
collectively. Go through each bill's itemised breakdown once — that's where
they live, not on the summary page you normally see.

## 11. Give every bill an owner and a review date

Household bills drift because nobody is responsible for them. Put a single
recurring appointment in the calendar — one hour, twice a year — and work down
the list from step one.

Twice a year is enough. It catches every annual renewal before it rolls, and it
turns "we should look at that" into something that actually happens on a
specific Tuesday.

## 12. Move the saving somewhere you can't spend it

This is the step people skip, and skipping it undoes everything above. A bill
that drops by $18 a month doesn't make you $18 better off — it just makes your
current account slightly more comfortable, and comfortable money gets spent.

Set up a standing order for the exact amount you cut, on the day after payday,
into savings. Then the saving is real and you can point at it. If the total

## Common questions

### How much can you realistically save on monthly bills?

It varies enormously, but the pattern is consistent: the largest single win is
usually renegotiating broadband, mobile or insurance, and the rest comes from a
handful of small forgotten subscriptions. Households that have never done a
full review tend to find more than they expect on the first pass and much less
on the second.

### Is it worth calling to negotiate a bill?

For anything on a contract with competitors — broadband, mobile, TV, insurance
— yes, and it's usually the best-paid fifteen minutes of the month. Providers
budget for retention discounts. The one thing that makes it work is being
genuinely willing to switch, because the discount exists to stop you.

### Should I cancel subscriptions or just pause them?

Pause if the service offers it and you know you'll return, cancel otherwise. The
risk with pausing is that it restarts silently — so if you pause, put the
restart date in your calendar the same minute, exactly as you would with an
introductory rate.

### What's the fastest way to see all my recurring payments?

Search three months of statements for the same amount appearing on the same
date, and check the subscriptions page in your app store, which holds the ones
that never show a recognisable name on a bank statement. A
[budget spreadsheet](https://www.digital-planning-studio.com/#aff=tamirjr)
keeps the list in one place so next year's review takes minutes instead of an
evening.
