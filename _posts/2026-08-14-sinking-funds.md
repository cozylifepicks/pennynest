A budget rarely breaks because of a surprise. It breaks in December, or the
month the car needs tyres, or the week the insurance renews — costs you knew
were coming and simply had no money set aside for. They arrive once a year, so
they never make it onto a monthly budget, and then they land all at once.

A sinking fund is the fix, and it is far less serious than it sounds. You take
a cost you know is coming, divide it by the number of months until it arrives,
and put that much aside every month. By the time the bill lands, it is already
paid for. Nothing about your income has to change — only the timing.

Here are the twelve steps we'd work through, in the order that gets you the
biggest relief soonest.

## 1. Write down every bill that ambushed you in the last year

Start with history, not theory. Go back through twelve months of statements and
write down every payment over about fifty pounds or dollars that was not a
normal monthly cost. Car service. Insurance renewal. Christmas. The vet. The
school trip. The boiler.

You are not budgeting yet — you are just building the list. Most people find
between six and ten, and are surprised by the total. That total is the number
that has been wrecking a month at a time all year.

## 2. Put a real number and a real month next to each one

Next to each item, write what it actually cost and roughly when it landed. Use
last year's figure and add a little; prices rarely fall. If you genuinely
cannot remember, guess high — a fund that overshoots is a good problem.

The month matters as much as the amount. A cost due in eleven months needs a
much smaller monthly contribution than one due in two, and the whole method
falls apart if everything is treated as equally urgent.

## 3. Divide by the months you actually have left

This is the entire calculation. Take the cost, divide by the number of months
until it is due, and that is your monthly contribution to that fund. A £600 car
service ten months away is £60 a month. Christmas at £400 with five months to go
is £80.

Do it for every item on the list and add the monthly numbers up. That total is
what your budget has been missing all along. It usually looks alarming the first
time — which is the point, because you have been paying it anyway, just in
painful lumps.

## 4. Start with the three that hurt most, not all ten

Almost nobody can fund ten pots at once from a budget that was already tight.
Rank the list by how much damage each one did when it landed, and start the top
three. The rest can wait a month or two.

Three funded properly beats ten funded a bit. Partial funds tend to get raided,
because a pot that will not cover the bill anyway feels like ordinary savings.

## 5. Move the money on payday, before anything else

Do the transfer the day the money lands, not at the end of the month with
whatever is left. There is never anything left. The
[payday routine](/payday-routine/) is twenty minutes and this is the part of it
that pays for itself fastest.

Automate it if your bank allows standing orders between your own accounts. A
fund you have to remember to feed is a fund you will stop feeding by March.

## 6. Give every fund a name, out loud

"Savings" gets spent. "Car tyres, January" does not. Naming a pot for its actual
job is a small thing that changes how it feels to take money out of it — you are
no longer dipping into savings, you are stealing from the car.

This is the same mechanism that makes the [cash envelope
method](/cash-envelope-method/) work, applied to costs that are months away
instead of weeks.

## 7. Keep the funds out of your current account

Money that sits alongside your spending money gets spent, however well labelled.
Move sinking funds to a separate savings account, a second bank, or physical
envelopes — anywhere that requires one deliberate step to reach.

The friction is the feature. You want it to be possible to get the money out,
because sometimes you genuinely need to, but you want it to take a minute of
thought first.

## 8. Round every monthly number up

If the maths says £47, put in £50. If it says £83, put in £90. Rounding up costs
a few pounds a month and covers the thing that breaks most sinking funds: the
bill being bigger this year than last.

It also means you finish slightly early, which is the most motivating thing
that can happen to a savings pot in month three.

## 9. Add one fund for "something will break"

Every list of known costs is incomplete. The washing machine is not on your
list. Neither is the phone screen or the emergency dentist. Run one general
fund alongside the named ones for exactly this.

It is not the same as an emergency fund covering lost income — it is smaller and
it gets used. Ours is topped up first and raided most, and it has kept every
other pot intact. If you have neither yet, build this one first and read
[12 ways to save your first $1,000](/save-your-first-1000/) for the bigger
version.

## 10. Refill a fund the month after you empty it

The moment a fund does its job, it is empty and the clock restarts. Work out the
new monthly number that same week, while the relief of not putting it on a card
is still fresh.

This is where most people quietly stop. The bill got paid, the pressure lifted,
and the next twelve months of contributions never got set up — so next year is a
crisis again.

## 11. Review the whole list once a year, on a fixed date

Pick a date you will not forget — the first weekend of January, or your birthday
— and re-run the list. Costs change, some funds are no longer needed, and new
ones appear the moment you get a car, a pet or a child.

Twenty minutes once a year is the entire maintenance cost of this system. Put it
in the calendar now, while you are thinking about it.

## 12. Let a fund close when its job is done

When the car is sold or the course is paid for, close the fund and move its
monthly contribution somewhere else — another pot, or savings proper. Do it
deliberately, in the same sitting.

Left alone, that money silently returns to general spending and you will never
notice where it went. A closed fund should feel like a small pay rise, because
that is exactly what it is.

## Common questions

### What is a sinking fund, in plain English?

It is money you save a bit at a time for a specific cost you know is coming —
Christmas, the car service, the insurance renewal — so that when the bill
arrives it is already covered. The name only means the balance sinks back to
zero when it does its job, then builds up again.

### How many sinking funds should I have?

Start with three and add more once those are running comfortably. There is no
prize for having ten pots; there is a real cost to having ten that are each a
third full, because a fund that cannot cover its bill tends to get spent on
something else.

### What is the difference between a sinking fund and an emergency fund?

A sinking fund is for a cost you know about and can date. An emergency fund is
for the ones you cannot — a job loss, a big medical bill — and it is meant to
sit untouched. Most households need both, but sinking funds are the ones that
stop the ordinary months going wrong.

### Where should I keep sinking fund money?

Anywhere that is separate from your day-to-day spending account and reachable
within a day or two. A second savings account with sub-pots is easiest; physical
[envelopes or jars](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cash+envelopes+budget+savings+jars&tag=tamirugc20-20)
work just as well and are more visible, which some people need. A
[budget spreadsheet](https://www.digital-planning-studio.com/#aff=tamirjr)
tracks the balances either way.
