The first client is hard. The first *price* is worse. Almost everyone picks a
number that feels safe, hears a yes far too quickly, and then spends the next
three months doing real work for pocket change — resenting a client who never
did anything wrong.

Undercharging isn't modesty. It's the fastest way to quit a side hustle that was
actually working, because low prices need volume, and volume is the thing you
don't have room for around a job. The fix isn't confidence. It's having a few
rules you decided in advance, when nobody was waiting for an answer.

## 1. Price the outcome, not the hour

Nobody buys ninety minutes. They buy a tidy inbox, a month of posts, a set of
photos that make their shop look open. When you quote hours you invite a
negotiation about speed, and you get punished for being good — the faster you
work, the less you earn. Quote the finished thing, and getting quicker becomes
a raise instead of a pay cut.

## 2. Decide the number before the conversation

The worst prices in the world get invented live, in a message, while someone is
waiting. Write your price down before you ever open the chat — on paper, at the
kitchen table, with nobody watching. Then the conversation is just reading out a
decision you already made calmly, which is a completely different job from
inventing one under pressure.

## 3. The number that makes you slightly uncomfortable is usually right

If saying your price out loud feels totally relaxed, it's too low. The useful
signal is a small flinch — the one where you'd be quietly pleased if they said
yes. That flinch is not arrogance; it's the gap between what the work is worth
and what you're used to being paid, and it closes with practice, not with
discounts.

## 4. Charge a flat price for anything you've done before

The first time you do something, hourly protects you. The second time, it robs
you. Once you've done a job once, you know roughly how long it takes, so quote
one flat number for the whole thing. Clients strongly prefer it too — a fixed
price is a decision they can make once, while an hourly rate is an open meter
they have to keep worrying about.

## 5. Add up your real hours, including the invisible ones

The reason a "decent" rate still feels like nothing is that you only counted the
work. Add the messages, the two rounds of changes, the travel, the twenty
minutes finding the file again. A job that felt like three hours is usually
five, and pricing the three is exactly how a fair-looking rate turns into
minimum wage by Friday.

## 6. Price the rush, not the panic

"Can you do it by tomorrow?" is a different product from "can you do it this
month". A rush costs you an evening you'd planned to spend elsewhere, so it
should carry a visible premium — a flat surcharge is simplest. Most people say
yes without blinking, because urgency is exactly when they're least
price-sensitive, and the ones who say no were never really in a rush.

## 7. Never discount before you're asked

The most expensive sentence in a side hustle is "…but I could do it for less."
Offered before anyone objected, it tells the client two things you didn't mean:
that the first number was made up, and that any number is negotiable. Say the
price, then stop talking. Silence is not a rejection — it's usually someone
doing arithmetic.

## 8. Offer three options instead of one

One price is a yes-or-no. Three is a which-one, and that question is far easier
to answer. Give a small version, the one you actually want to sell, and a bigger
one — most people take the middle, and the expensive option quietly makes the
middle look sensible. It also stops "too expensive" from ending the conversation,
because there's somewhere smaller to land.

## 9. Put the price in writing before you start

Not a contract — a message. What you'll deliver, by when, for how much, and what
counts as an extra. Almost every ugly money conversation in a side hustle comes
from two people remembering a spoken number differently, and a three-line
message written on the day everyone is still happy prevents nearly all of them.

## 10. Ask for half up front

Half before, half on delivery is completely normal and almost nobody argues with
it. It filters out the people who were never going to pay, it means a job that
falls apart doesn't cost you the whole month, and it puts a small amount of
commitment on both sides. Ask for it in the same message as the price, so it
reads as your standard process rather than a reaction to them.

## 11. Raise it on the next client, not this one

Re-pricing a happy client mid-job is stressful and rarely worth it. Raising the
number for the *next* person costs nothing, risks nothing you already have, and
you find out immediately whether the market minded. Do it every few clients
until someone hesitates — that hesitation is the only honest market research
available to you, and it's free.

## 12. Check what each job actually paid per hour

At the end of a job, divide what you were paid by the hours it really took. Not
to feel bad — to find the pattern. Almost everyone discovers that one type of
work quietly pays double another, and the whole point of a side hustle with
limited hours is to notice that and do more of the good one.

## Common questions

### How much should I charge for my first client?

Enough that you'd be pleased to do it again. A common starting point is what
you'd earn per hour at a job you'd be willing to do, then rounded up for the fact
that you're carrying your own tax, tools and admin. Don't quote a "beginner
discount" — it sets a ceiling you'll fight for months.

### Should I charge hourly or a flat rate?

Hourly for the first one of anything, when you genuinely don't know how long it
takes. Flat for everything after that, because you now do — and a flat price
rewards you for getting faster instead of punishing you for it.

### What do I say when someone says it's too expensive?

Don't drop the price. Offer a smaller version instead: fewer items, a longer
deadline, less revision. That keeps the rate intact and hands them a real choice.
If nothing fits, it was a budget mismatch, not a rejection of you.

### How do I raise my prices without losing clients?

Raise them on new clients first and leave existing ones alone for a while. When
you do raise an existing rate, give notice, keep it simple, and don't apologise
or over-explain — a short, calm message about a new rate from next month is
accepted far more often than people expect.

## The takeaway

Underpricing is rarely a confidence problem — it's a decision made in the wrong
moment, under pressure, with no rule to fall back on. Pick your number at the
kitchen table, quote the outcome, put it in writing, ask for half up front, and
check afterwards what it really paid per hour. Then raise it on the next one.
